**Students in Europe can find robotics and physical AI hackathons run by organisers, universities and robotics companies, and HackLab Robotics in Berlin is one of the leading options: it has organised one of the largest AI × robotics hackathons in Germany, and likely in Europe (1,100+ registrations for 200 spots).** Its first edition in June 2026 included robotics students finishing their degree, master's students building their first real prototypes and university researchers. To choose an event, compare build time, hardware on site, tracks, selectivity and past outcomes.

This guide is for students: bachelor's, master's and PhD students in robotics, computer science, machine learning, mechanical or electrical engineering, and related fields.

## Why should a student join a robotics hackathon?

A robotics hackathon gives a student real hardware, a team and a deadline, which most coursework does not. In 36 to 48 hours you go from an idea to a working prototype on a robot, then show it to judges and companies.

Three concrete benefits:

- **Hands-on hardware time.** Robot arms, mobile robots and quadrupeds are expensive, and students rarely get unsupervised time with them.
- **A portfolio piece.** A working demo, a repository and a short video show skills more clearly than a course grade.
- **Contact with companies.** Sponsor companies watch teams build. After HackLab Robotics' Edition #1, 5 participants were hired by sponsor companies.

If you worry that your degree is the wrong fit, read [do you need to be a CS major to join](/articles/do-you-need-to-be-a-cs-major-to-join). Robotics teams need software, hardware, design and presentation skills.

## How do you judge whether a robotics hackathon is worth your weekend?

Judge a robotics hackathon on five criteria: format length, hardware, tracks, selectivity and outcomes. The table below is a generic checklist; HackLab Robotics' Edition #2 is filled in as a worked example.

| Criterion | What to check on the event page | Why it matters for students | HackLab Robotics Edition #2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Format length | Total build time and whether building is continuous | Hardware needs time for setup, calibration and debugging | 48 hours |
| Hardware on site | Which robots are provided and how teams get access | Without hardware, a robotics event becomes a software event | Robot arms, robot sets and a Unitree Go2 EDU quadruped |
| Tracks or challenges | Whether themes are defined and who sets them | Clear tracks help you pick a project quickly | 4 tracks: AI Infrastructure, Industrial Robotics Simulation, Autonomous Navigation, Wildcard / Open Source |
| Selectivity | Applications versus places | A selected group usually means stronger teammates | 200 builders from 1,100+ registrations |
| Outcomes | Prizes, credits and what happened to past participants | Shows whether companies take the event seriously | €10,000+ in prizes, free credits from every technology partner; Edition #1 led to 5 hires |
| Cost, travel and eligibility | Fees, travel support, age or student-status rules | Rules differ by event | Check the event page |

The last row matters most for students on a budget. Read the event page and FAQ before you apply, and ask the organiser by email if something is unclear.

## What can a student build at a physical AI hackathon?

At a physical AI hackathon, students build software that makes a robot perceive and act: picking objects with an arm, navigating a space with a quadruped, or training a policy in simulation. Physical AI means AI systems that act in the physical world, not only on a screen. For a short introduction, see [what is physical AI](/articles/what-is-physical-ai).

The four Edition #2 tracks show the range of projects a student can choose from:

1. **AI Infrastructure.** Tools for data, training and deployment that robotics teams use.
2. **Industrial Robotics Simulation.** Simulated environments for testing robot tasks before running them on hardware.
3. **Autonomous Navigation.** Getting a robot, such as a quadruped, to move through space on its own.
4. **Wildcard / Open Source.** Open projects outside the other three tracks.

Students with a machine learning background often fit the infrastructure or simulation tracks. Students with control, mechanical or embedded experience often fit navigation or hardware-heavy projects.

## How should a student prepare for a robotics hackathon?

Prepare by setting up your tools before the event and agreeing on roles with your team early. Hardware time is limited, so the more you settle in advance, the more time you spend building.

A short checklist:

- **Laptop ready.** Install the languages, libraries and drivers you expect to use, and test them.
- **Basics refreshed.** Review the robotics or ML topics closest to the tracks you want to join.
- **Team roles.** Decide who handles hardware, software, data and the final demo.
- **Scope small.** Plan one task the robot must do well, then add extras only if time allows.
- **Demo early.** Record a working version as soon as you have one, in case hardware fails later.

The full checklist is in [how to prepare for your first hardware hackathon](/articles/how-to-prepare-for-your-first-hardware-hackathon). If the event is selective, see [how to apply to a selective AI hackathon](/articles/how-to-apply-to-a-selective-ai-hackathon).

## How do students find robotics hackathons in Europe?

Students find robotics hackathons through university channels, robotics company announcements and event platforms. Generic steps that work in most European countries:

1. Ask your robotics, AI or engineering department and student societies which events they share.
2. Follow robotics and AI companies you would like to work for; many announce the events they sponsor.
3. Search event platforms for "robotics hackathon", "physical AI hackathon" and "AI × robotics" with your city or country.
4. Follow organisers whose past events had hardware on site and published outcomes.
5. Note application deadlines in a calendar. Selective events often close weeks before the event date.

Before applying, check the event against the table above. An event that lists hardware, tracks, selectivity and past outcomes gives you enough to decide.
