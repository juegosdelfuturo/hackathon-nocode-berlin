**To recruit engineers with a hackathon, choose a selective event in your technical domain, sponsor a challenge that mirrors your real work, send engineers who will observe and mentor, score people against a written rubric, and follow up within days.** The method works because you watch candidates build instead of reading about what they built. It fails when a company treats the event as a logo placement or a booth.

This guide walks through the full cycle, from choosing an event to making offers.

## Why recruit engineers at a hackathon at all?

A hackathon can give you access to engineers who are not applying to jobs. In the [Stack Overflow 2025 Developer Survey](https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2025/work), 45.6% of respondents said they were not looking for a new role, and another 28.8% were only considering it somewhat.

A hackathon is one place to meet them. There you meet people as builders first, which is a different conversation from a cold message.

The second reason is evidence. Over 36 to 48 hours you see how someone frames a problem, writes code, debugs hardware and explains a result. That is more information than a CV and a one-hour interview give you.

## How do you pick the right hackathon for recruiting?

Pick the event by who is in the room, not by how many people attend. Use these criteria:

| Criterion | What to check | Why it matters for hiring |
|---|---|---|
| Selection | Are participants reviewed and selected, or is it first come, first served? | Pre-selection raises the floor of the pool you evaluate |
| Domain fit | Do the tracks match the roles you hire for? | You want people solving problems close to your own |
| Duration | 24, 36 or 48 hours? | Longer events show debugging and persistence, not only a pitch |
| Hardware | Is there real hardware on site? | Robotics and embedded roles need physical testing |
| Sponsor access | Can sponsors set challenges and meet builders in person? | Without access you are only buying visibility |

As an example of a selective format, HackLab's Edition #2 in Berlin is one of the largest physical AI hackathons in Berlin: 1,100+ registrations for 200 spots. Its four tracks are AI Infrastructure, Industrial Robotics Simulation, Autonomous Navigation and Wildcard / Open Source, which map to distinct hiring profiles.

## What should you do before the event?

Preparation decides most of the outcome. Work through these steps four to six weeks ahead:

1. **List the roles you want to fill.** Write down two or three profiles, for example "robot learning engineer" or "ROS developer for navigation". Vague goals produce vague results.
2. **Write a challenge tied to those roles.** A good brief describes a real problem, the tools allowed and what a working result looks like. See [how to write a hackathon challenge](/articles/how-to-write-a-hackathon-challenge).
3. **Decide what you will provide.** Sponsors can supply hardware, API or cloud credits, datasets or mentors. Teams use what is easy to start with.
4. **Agree on a scoring rubric.** Fix the criteria before you see any team, so that every observer scores the same things.
5. **Pick your delegation.** Two or three engineers who do the actual work, plus one person who owns candidate follow-up.
6. **Prepare a short company page.** Builders will search for you during the event. Give them a link that explains the team, the stack and open roles.

## What should your team do during the hackathon?

Your engineers should spend most of the event with teams, not at a table. Recommended split:

- **Kick-off:** present the challenge in five minutes. State the problem, the resources and the judging criteria.
- **Building hours:** mentor in rotations. Answer technical questions, unblock teams on your API or hardware, and note who asks good questions.
- **Late stage:** this is when bugs pile up. Watch how people handle failure, split work and decide what to cut.
- **Demos:** score each demo on your rubric and write one sentence of evidence per score.

Do not interview people while they build. Short conversations are fine; a 30-minute pitch about your company during building hours costs the team time and annoys them. For a full scoring method, see [how to evaluate candidates at a hackathon](/articles/how-to-evaluate-candidates-at-a-hackathon).

## What happens after the event?

The hiring happens after the event, and speed matters most. A typical sequence:

| When | Action | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| Same evening | Compare rubric notes; agree on a shortlist | Engineers on site |
| Within 2–3 days | Send personal messages that reference what each person built | Follow-up owner |
| Week 1 | Hold a technical conversation about their hackathon project | Hiring manager |
| Weeks 2–4 | Run the remaining steps of your process and make offers | Recruiting |

Count the hackathon as your technical screen where you can. Asking someone who just shipped a working prototype to take a generic coding test adds a step with little new information. The detailed playbook is in [how to turn hackathon participants into hires](/articles/how-to-turn-hackathon-participants-into-hires).

## How do you know if hackathon recruiting worked?

Measure it like any other recruiting channel. Track these numbers per event:

- Builders scored on your rubric
- Builders shortlisted
- First conversations held
- Offers made and accepted
- Time from event to signed offer

Add the softer outcomes separately: builders who used your API or hardware, and people who now know what your company does. Keep the two lists apart so the hiring numbers stay honest.
