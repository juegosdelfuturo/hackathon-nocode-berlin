**To turn hackathon participants into hires, agree on a shortlist the evening the event ends, send personal messages within two to three days, use the hackathon as your technical round, and run the remaining steps fast enough to make offers within a few weeks.** The event gives you evidence and goodwill. Both fade quickly if nobody follows up.

This is the playbook for the days and weeks after the demos.

## Why does the follow-up decide whether you hire?

Because the hackathon only produces candidates; the follow-up produces hires. HackLab's Edition #1 shows the path exists: 5 participants were hired by sponsor companies after the event. Each of those hires required someone to act after the demos ended.

Slow or messy processes lose people. In a 2022 Stack Overflow Knows survey of 2,600+ tech professionals, [34% named a disorganized interview and 25% a lengthy hiring process as reasons they stopped pursuing a job](https://stackoverflow.blog/2022/12/15/job-insights-from-the-tech-community-the-latest-survey-results-from-stack-overflow-knows/). A hackathon candidate who has just met several companies has alternatives.

## What is the timeline from demo day to offer?

Plan the full sequence before the event, so nobody has to invent it on Monday morning.

| Day | Step | Goal |
|---|---|---|
| Day 0 (demo evening) | Calibrate scores, agree on a shortlist | Clear list of people to contact |
| Day 1–3 | Send personal first messages | Book a first conversation |
| Week 1 | Project deep-dive with the hiring manager | Fill the gaps the event did not show |
| Week 2 | Remaining steps: team meeting, references if used | Decision |
| Weeks 2–4 | Offer and negotiation | Signed offer |

If your normal process takes longer, compress it for this group. You already have the strongest technical evidence most candidates will ever give you.

## What should you collect during the event to make follow-up possible?

Collect permission and context, not just names. Before the demos end:

- **Ask each shortlisted builder** whether you may contact them about roles, and by which channel.
- **Write down what they built** and which part was theirs. This becomes the first line of your message.
- **Note their situation:** finishing a degree, already employed, open to relocating, needing a visa. It changes the offer you can make.
- **Tell them what you will store and why.**

The last point is a legal requirement, not only good manners. The [European Commission's summary of the GDPR principles](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/information-business-and-organisations/principles-gdpr_en) states that data may be collected "only for a specific purpose" which must be told to the person, and that the information you give must include, among other things, the purpose, the legal basis and how long the data will be stored. Ask your data protection officer how this applies to your event notes.

## What should the first message say?

The first message should prove you were paying attention. Keep it short and specific:

1. **Who you are** and that you were at the event.
2. **What you saw them do,** in one concrete sentence. "Your team's grasping pipeline recovered after the camera calibration failed" beats "great project".
3. **Why it matters to you:** the team, the problem or the role it connects to.
4. **One next step,** with two or three time options for a 30-minute call.

Avoid sending the same template to the whole shortlist.

## How should you change your interview process for hackathon candidates?

Remove the steps the hackathon already covered and keep the ones it did not. A practical split:

| Interview step | Keep or drop for hackathon candidates |
|---|---|
| CV screen | Drop; you saw the work |
| Generic coding test | Usually drop; the build replaced it |
| Project deep-dive | Keep; ask why they made each decision |
| System design or domain depth | Keep; a 36-hour build rarely shows long-term design |
| Team and manager conversation | Keep |
| References | Keep if your policy requires them |

The project deep-dive is the most useful new step. Ask what they would change with another week, what failed and what they learned. For the scoring side of the event itself, see [how to evaluate candidates at a hackathon](/articles/how-to-evaluate-candidates-at-a-hackathon).

## What if the best builder is not ready to join now?

Keep the relationship, with their agreement. At HackLab's Edition #1, attendees included robotics students finishing their degree, master's students and university researchers, and some of them may not be able to start a full-time job straight away. Options:

- Offer an internship, a working-student contract or a thesis topic.
- Agree on a specific date to talk again, and put it in the calendar.
- Invite them to a team event or a demo day at your office.

Only keep their data for as long as you told them you would. Storing a contact list indefinitely "just in case" conflicts with the GDPR principle that data is kept no longer than necessary.

## What about candidates from outside Germany?

Start the paperwork conversation early. If a builder lives outside Germany or needs a work permit, that affects the start date and the offer. Ask about it in the first call, not after the offer. The routes are covered in [hiring international engineers in Germany](/articles/hiring-international-engineers-in-germany).

## What should you tell the people you do not hire?

Tell them clearly and quickly. A short, honest message with one specific piece of feedback keeps your reputation intact among builders who will attend future events and talk to each other. Participants who want to know what to do next can read [what happens after the hackathon](/articles/what-happens-after-the-hackathon).
