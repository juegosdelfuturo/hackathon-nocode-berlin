**Yes, Europe has a shortage of AI and tech talent: 57.5% of EU enterprises that tried to recruit ICT specialists in 2023 had difficulty filling the roles, and the EU has reached only about half of its 2030 target of 20 million ICT specialists.** Robotics engineers are not counted separately in official statistics, but their roles combine software, AI and hardware skills, so they draw on the scarcest parts of this pool. The general IT market has eased in some countries, yet specialised roles remain hard to fill.

## What do official EU statistics say about the shortage?

EU statistics show that most companies that hire tech specialists struggle to do so. According to [Eurostat's data on hard-to-fill vacancies](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=ICT_specialists_-_statistics_on_hard-to-fill_vacancies_in_enterprises), 9.55% of EU enterprises recruited or tried to recruit ICT specialists in 2023, and 57.5% of those reported difficulties filling the vacancies. In Germany the figure was 72.41%, well above the EU average.

| Indicator (Eurostat, reference year 2023) | Figure |
|---|---|
| EU enterprises that recruited or tried to recruit ICT specialists | 9.55% |
| Large enterprises that recruited or tried to recruit ICT specialists | 51.87% |
| Small enterprises that recruited or tried to recruit ICT specialists | 6.23% |
| Recruiting enterprises with hard-to-fill ICT vacancies, EU | 57.5% |
| Recruiting enterprises with hard-to-fill ICT vacancies, Germany | 72.41% |

Eurostat notes that enterprises faced the same difficulties regardless of their size. Startups and corporates compete for the same people.

## How far is the EU from its digital talent target?

The EU is about halfway to its 2030 goal. The [EU Digital Skills and Jobs Platform](https://digital-skills-jobs.europa.eu/en/latest/briefs/digital-experts-deep-dive-0) states the Digital Decade target of 20 million employed ICT specialists by 2030, against around 9 million in 2022. [Eurostat's employment data](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=ICT_specialists_in_employment) shows more than 10 million ICT specialists employed in the EU in 2025, after growth of 59.4% over ten years, compared with 9.8% for total employment.

Growth is fast, but the gap is still just under 10 million people with five years to go. At the same time, many companies expect AI adoption to add demand for engineers with AI skills, as the Bitkom data below shows.

## Is the shortage easing?

In Germany's general IT market, yes, but not for every role. A [Bitkom study from September 2026](https://www.bitkom.org/Presse/Presseinformation/IT-Fachkraeftemangel-in-den-letzten-drei-Jahren-halbiert) counts 79,000 unfilled IT positions in Germany, down from 109,000 in 2025 and 149,000 in 2023. At the same time:

- 78% of companies still observe a shortage of IT specialists.
- 76% expect the shortage to worsen.
- 49% expect AI to increase demand for IT specialists with AI knowledge.

The headline number has fallen, but the share of companies that feel the shortage remains high, and AI is shifting demand towards more specialised profiles.

## Why are robotics and physical AI roles harder to fill than general tech roles?

Robotics and physical AI roles need a combination of skills that few people have. A typical role mixes:

1. **Software engineering** in C++ and Python, often with real-time constraints.
2. **Machine learning**, increasingly including robot learning from demonstrations or simulation.
3. **Hardware understanding**: sensors, actuators, calibration and safety.
4. **Systems integration** across all of the above.

Each skill alone is in demand; the combination is rare.

Demand for the underlying machines keeps growing as well. The [International Federation of Robotics](https://ifr.org/ifr-press-releases/news/global-robot-demand-in-factories-doubles-over-10-years) reports 85,000 industrial robots installed in Europe in 2024, the second-highest annual total on record, and 542,000 worldwide, more than double the number ten years earlier.

Each installation needs people who can integrate, program and improve it. For the specialised end of this market, see the [embodied AI talent market in 2026](/articles/embodied-ai-talent-market-2026).

## Does AI reduce the need for robotics and AI engineers?

No, so far AI has mostly changed which engineers companies look for rather than reducing how many they need. In the same Bitkom survey, 85% of German companies with IT positions said they had not cut IT jobs because of AI. Among companies that have cut or plan to cut IT positions, 35% said AI is replacing vacant roles they cannot fill, and 19% of companies with IT positions use AI more heavily to cover their need for skilled workers.

For robotics teams the effect is similar. AI coding tools speed up routine software work, but they do not calibrate a camera, tune a controller on a real arm or decide why a policy fails on hardware. Those tasks still need engineers with hands-on robotics experience.

## Which robotics and AI roles are hardest to fill?

The hardest roles to fill are the ones that cross disciplines. Based on the skill combinations above, these are the roles where the candidate pool is smallest:

- **Robot learning engineers** who can train policies and deploy them on real hardware.
- **Perception engineers** with production experience on real sensors rather than benchmark datasets.
- **Robotics software engineers** with real-time and safety experience.
- **AI infrastructure engineers** who understand robot data, not just text or images.
- **Technical leads** who have shipped a complete robot system.

Generalist software or data roles next to the robotics team are usually easier to fill and can free specialists to focus on the robot.

## What can companies do about the talent shortage?

Companies that hire well despite the shortage change their channels, not just their salaries. Six practical steps:

| Step | Why it helps |
|---|---|
| Source passive candidates directly | Most experienced engineers are already employed |
| Hire internationally and support relocation | Widens the pool beyond one country |
| Hire juniors and train them | Builds capacity that the market cannot supply |
| Separate must-have from learnable skills | Avoids rejecting strong candidates for one missing tool |
| Use channels where candidates build | Shows real ability and reaches engineers who ignore job ads |
| Move fast once you find a fit | Other employers are competing for the same person |

Hiring in a strong local hub also helps. The article on [why Berlin works for robotics and AI hiring](/articles/why-berlin-for-robotics-and-ai-hiring) covers one example.
