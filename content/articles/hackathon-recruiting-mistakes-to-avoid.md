**The most common hackathon recruiting mistakes are going without target roles, sending recruiters instead of engineers, setting a challenge teams cannot use, judging only the winners, and following up too late.** Each one turns a weekend with strong builders into a weekend of brand exposure with no hires. All of them are cheap to fix before the event.

Below are ten mistakes, why they cost you candidates and what to do instead.

## Mistake 1: Why is going without target roles a problem?

Without target roles, your team does not know whom to watch. Everyone looks impressive at a demo, and you leave with ten "interesting people" and no decision.

**Fix:** write down two or three role profiles before the event, with the skills each needs. Give the list to everyone you send.

## Mistake 2: Why is sending only recruiters a mistake?

Recruiters cannot judge a ROS launch file or a policy that only works in simulation, and builders know it. Technical people want technical conversations.

**Fix:** send engineers who would work with the hire, plus one person who owns follow-up. Two or three people who rotate are enough.

## Mistake 3: What is wrong with a vague or unusable challenge?

A challenge that is vague, needs data teams cannot access or requires a week of setup will be ignored. Teams choose what they can build on in the time available.

**Fix:** describe one real problem, the tools allowed and what "working" looks like. Test the setup yourself in under an hour. A full method is in [how to write a hackathon challenge](/articles/how-to-write-a-hackathon-challenge).

## Mistake 4: Why does it matter if your resources are hard to use?

If your API keys, credits or hardware take hours to activate, teams switch to something else and you lose both adoption and contact.

**Fix:** prepare credits and accounts before the event, write a one-page quick start and keep an engineer available to unblock teams. See [providing API credits to hackathon teams](/articles/providing-api-credits-to-hackathon-teams).

## Mistake 5: Why is judging only the winning team a mistake?

Prize judging and hiring answer different questions. Judges rank team results; you need individuals who fit your role. At HackLab's Edition #1, close to 20 final solutions were demoed to judges and sponsor companies, and each of those teams is a group of individuals to assess, whatever its final ranking.

**Fix:** score every team you observed, and every person within it, against your own rubric.

## Mistake 6: What goes wrong without a scoring rubric?

Without a rubric, scores follow presentation skill and the opinion of the most senior person in the room. Two observers watching the same team will remember different things.

**Fix:** agree on five or six criteria and a 1–4 scale before the event, record one line of evidence per score and compare notes the same evening. The method is in [how to evaluate candidates at a hackathon](/articles/how-to-evaluate-candidates-at-a-hackathon).

## Mistake 7: Why are long pitches during building time a problem?

Builders have a deadline. A 20-minute company presentation at a team's table takes time from them and is remembered for the wrong reasons.

**Fix:** pitch in the kick-off, keep table conversations short and offer fixed office hours where builders can come to you.

## Mistake 8: Why is ignoring the code of conduct a risk?

Sponsors are part of the event's community, and some events say so explicitly. [Major League Hacking's code of conduct](https://github.com/MLH/mlh-policies/blob/main/code-of-conduct.md), for example, states that "sponsors, judges, mentors, volunteers, organizers, MLH staff, and anyone else participating in the event" are subject to its anti-harassment policy at its events.

**Fix:** read the code of conduct of the event you attend, brief everyone you send and name one person on your team as the contact if something goes wrong.

## Mistake 9: Why is slow follow-up the most expensive mistake?

Because by the time you write, the builder may have already talked to other companies at the same event. Interest built over a weekend fades if nobody acts on it.

**Fix:** agree on a shortlist on the last evening and send personal messages within two to three days. Reference what each person built. Do not send a generic template to the whole list.

## Mistake 10: Why is measuring only hires on the day a mistake?

The hiring work starts after the event: conversations, interviews and offers take weeks. If you judge the weekend on Monday, you will call it a failure before that work has started.

**Fix:** track the full funnel over the following weeks: builders scored, contacted, interviewed, offered and hired. Report tool adoption and brand outcomes separately.

## What do these mistakes and fixes look like side by side?

A summary for your event checklist:

| Mistake | Cost | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| No target roles | No clear shortlist | Two or three written role profiles |
| Only recruiters attend | No technical credibility | Engineers plus one follow-up owner |
| Unusable challenge | Teams ignore it | One real problem, tested setup |
| Hard-to-use resources | No adoption, no contact | Prepared credits, quick start, on-site help |
| Judging only winners | Missed individuals | Score every person you observed |
| No rubric | Biased, inconsistent scores | Fixed criteria, evidence, calibration |
| Long pitches during building | Annoyed teams | Kick-off pitch and office hours |
| Ignoring the code of conduct | Reputational and personal risk | Read it and brief your team |
| Slow follow-up | Lost candidates | Messages within 2–3 days |
| Measuring on day one | Wrong conclusions | Track the funnel for weeks |
