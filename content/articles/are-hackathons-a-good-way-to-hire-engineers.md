**Hackathons are a good way to hire engineers for specialist technical roles, provided the event pre-selects participants and your own engineers watch the work. They show practical skill under real constraints better than a CV does, but they are less standardised than a formal assessment and do not replace the rest of a hiring process.** Use them as a strong first stage, not as the whole decision.

This article weighs the evidence for and against the channel.

## What does research say about assessing candidates through real work?

Research on personnel selection supports methods close to the actual job. SIOP, the professional society for industrial-organisational psychology, [summarised a 2022 meta-analytic re-analysis by Sackett and colleagues](https://www.siop.org/tip-article/is-cognitive-ability-the-best-predictor-of-job-performance) with these operational validity estimates:

| Selection method | Mean operational validity (r) |
|---|---|
| Structured interviews | .42 |
| Job knowledge tests | .40 |
| Work sample tests | .33 |
| Cognitive ability tests | .31 |

The same summary notes that structured interviews showed a relatively high spread around their mean.

## Does that research apply to hackathons?

Only partly. A hackathon looks like a work sample, because people build real things under time pressure. It is not a validated work sample test, and the SIOP summary gives no estimate for hackathons.

The differences matter:

- **Tasks differ.** Each team chooses its own project inside a track.
- **Teams differ.** A strong engineer on a weak team can look worse than they are, and the reverse.
- **Conditions differ.** Some teams get stuck on hardware for hours; others never touch it.

The practical conclusion: treat the hackathon as rich evidence, then add structure. Score people on a fixed rubric and follow up with a structured conversation about what they built.

## What are the strengths of hiring through hackathons?

The main strength is that you see behaviour, not claims. In detail:

- **Real work over long periods.** Companies see candidates build working prototypes in 36–48 hours instead of reading CVs.
- **Hard-to-fake skills.** Debugging, prioritising and cooperating under pressure show up within hours.
- **Access outside job boards.** You meet builders in a context other than an application, including people who have not applied to you.
- **Two-way evaluation.** Candidates see how your engineers work, which helps them decide too.
- **Side benefits.** Builders try your tools, and your engineers get a weekend of product feedback.

## What are the limits?

The limits are volume, standardisation and access. Be honest about each:

| Limit | What it means | How to reduce it |
|---|---|---|
| Lower volume | Fewer candidates than job boards or fairs | Use hackathons for a few specialist roles |
| Team effects | Individual contributions are harder to see | Ask each person what they built; observe at fixed points |
| Uneven conditions | Tasks and luck differ between teams | Score process, not only the final result |
| Who can attend | Favours people free for a full weekend | Keep other channels open for other candidates |
| Domain narrowness | Shows a slice of skills | Add a design or domain conversation afterwards |
| Staffing cost | Your engineers spend the weekend there | Rotate a small team of two or three |

None of these makes the channel weak. They define where it fits.

## When is a hackathon a good hiring channel, and when is it not?

It is a good channel when the role is technical and the event matches it. Use this checklist:

**Good fit:**
- Specialist roles such as robot learning, simulation, autonomous navigation or ML infrastructure
- Roles where hands-on hardware or prototyping skill matters
- An event that reviews and selects applicants
- Your engineers can attend for most of the event

**Poor fit:**
- High-volume hiring for similar roles, where [job fairs and campus channels](/articles/hackathons-vs-job-fairs-for-tech-recruiting) scale better
- Non-technical roles
- Senior leadership roles, where a weekend build says little about the job
- Events with open entry and no technical match to your work

## How does a hackathon compare with take-home tasks and interviews?

A hackathon gives more evidence per candidate but less control over the conditions. Each common method trades one against the other:

| Method | What it shows | Control over conditions | Candidate time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unstructured interview | Conversation skills, self-description | Low | About an hour |
| Structured interview | Answers to the same questions on a shared scale | High | About an hour |
| Take-home task | Individual work on a set problem | Medium | Several hours |
| Hackathon | Building, debugging and teamwork over 36–48 hours | Low to medium | A full weekend, by choice |

The useful combination is to use the hackathon for evidence and a structured conversation afterwards for comparability. Candidates who built at the event have already spent their weekend; avoid adding a take-home task on top.

## Is there real evidence that companies hire from hackathons?

Yes. After HackLab's Edition #1 in Berlin, a 36-hour event held on 20–21 June 2026, 5 participants were hired by sponsor companies. That shows the path works when companies are present and act on what they see. What the number does and does not tell you is covered in [five hires from one hackathon](/articles/five-hires-from-one-hackathon-hacklab-edition-1).

One event does not set a benchmark for your own results. Track your own numbers per event.

## How should you judge whether the channel worked for you?

Judge it on hires and on cost in engineer time, compared with your other channels. Keep a simple record for each event:

1. Builders scored on your rubric
2. Builders shortlisted and contacted
3. Offers made and accepted
4. Engineer hours spent on site and on follow-up

Then add the non-hiring returns, such as tool adoption, separately. A full method for this is in [how to measure hackathon sponsorship ROI](/articles/how-to-measure-hackathon-sponsorship-roi).
