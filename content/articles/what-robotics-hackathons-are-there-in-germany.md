<p><strong>Germany has few dedicated robotics hackathons, because physical builds need hardware, space and technical support that most software hackathons do not provide. One of the leading options is HackLab Robotics in Berlin, which has organised one of the largest AI × robotics hackathons in Germany, and likely in Europe: 1,100+ registrations for 200 spots (Edition #2).</strong> This guide is written for builders: students, engineers and researchers looking for a robotics or physical AI hackathon. It covers HackLab Robotics' two Berlin editions and how to find and judge other robotics hackathons yourself.</p>

<h2>Why are robotics hackathons rare in Germany?</h2>

<p>Most hackathons in Germany are software-only, because a laptop is all a software team needs. A robotics hackathon has to supply robots, sensors and spare parts, make room for hardware to move safely, and have people on hand who can help when a motor driver or a camera fails.</p>

<p>That makes them harder to organise and less frequent. When you do find one, the hardware on site is usually the main reason to go: few builders own a robot arm or a quadruped they can experiment with freely.</p>

<p>The demand is there. Berlin alone has over 130 robotics companies, according to Berlin Partner's <a href="https://businesslocationcenter.de/en/industry/robotik">Business Location Center</a>.</p>

<h2>How do you find robotics hackathons in Germany?</h2>

<p>Look in several places at once, because robotics hackathons are announced in scattered channels rather than on one list. Practical sources:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>University robotics departments and institutes.</strong> Their news pages, LinkedIn pages and student mailing lists often share events weeks before they spread elsewhere.</li>
<li><strong>Robotics and AI companies you follow.</strong> Companies that sponsor hackathons usually post about them, and their engineers repost them.</li>
<li><strong>Student robotics teams and lab groups.</strong> Members often hear about hardware events first and look for teammates.</li>
<li><strong>Online robotics communities.</strong> Chat servers and forums for robotics and physical AI developers share calls for participants.</li>
<li><strong>Event platforms.</strong> Search for "robotics", "physical AI", "embodied AI" and "hardware hackathon", not just "hackathon", and filter by city.</li>
</ol>

<p>Search in English and German, since announcements in Germany can be in either language.</p>

<h2>How do you evaluate a robotics hackathon before applying?</h2>

<p>Judge a robotics hackathon on what you will actually be able to build and who will see it. Use this checklist:</p>

<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Criterion</th><th>What to check</th><th>HackLab Robotics example</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Hardware on site</td><td>Are robots and sensors provided, or must you bring your own?</td><td>Robot arms, robot sets and a Unitree Go2 EDU quadruped (Edition #2)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Format length</td><td>Is there enough time for hardware to fail and be fixed?</td><td>36 hours (Edition #1), 48 hours (Edition #2)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Selectivity</td><td>Are participants selected, and from how many applicants?</td><td>130 of 600+ (Edition #1); 200 of 1,100+ (Edition #2)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Tracks</td><td>Are the challenges clear and relevant to your skills?</td><td>4 tracks, from AI Infrastructure to Autonomous Navigation</td></tr>
<tr><td>Companies present</td><td>Do sponsor companies build with you and watch the demos?</td><td>14 sponsor companies at Edition #1</td></tr>
<tr><td>Past outcomes</td><td>Did earlier participants get jobs, projects or contacts?</td><td>5 participants hired by sponsor companies after Edition #1</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<p>If an event cannot answer the first two rows, it is probably a software hackathon with a robotics theme. For the difference, see <a href="/articles/software-vs-hardware-hackathons">software vs. hardware hackathons</a>.</p>

<h2>Who gets selected for a robotics hackathon in Berlin?</h2>

<p>Selective robotics hackathons accept a mix of profiles, not only robotics specialists. Edition #1 participants included robotics students finishing their degree, people with machine learning backgrounds, robotics professionals across specialties, master's students building their first real prototypes, and university researchers.</p>

<p>With 600+ applications for 130 spots at Edition #1, a concrete application helps: link a project you built, name the hardware or models you have worked with, and say which track you would choose. For more, read <a href="/articles/how-to-apply-to-a-selective-ai-hackathon">how to apply to a selective AI hackathon</a>.</p>
