**To get accepted to a selective AI or robotics hackathon, make your application specific and checkable: link to things you have built, state what you want to build at the event, show what you would bring to a team, and apply early.** Selective events usually receive far more applications than places. HackLab Robotics, for example, selected 130 builders from 600+ applications for Edition #1 and 200 builders from 1,100+ registrations for Edition #2.

This guide is for builders: students, engineers and researchers applying to AI, machine learning or robotics hackathons. HackLab Robotics does not publish its exact selection criteria, so everything below is general good practice for selective events, not a description of any organiser's process.

## Why are some AI hackathons selective?

Some AI hackathons are selective because demand is higher than the number of places, hardware or mentors available. Robotics events have an extra limit: a fixed number of robots, and every team needs time on them.

The demand can be large. HackLab Robotics has organised one of the largest AI × robotics hackathons in Germany, and likely in Europe (1,100+ registrations for 200 spots). When an event is this oversubscribed, a generic application is easy to pass over.

## What do organisers of selective hackathons usually want to see?

Organisers of selective hackathons usually want evidence that you will build something, finish it and work well in a team. The table below lists common application elements and how to make each one strong. It is generic advice, not any organiser's scoring sheet.

| Application element | Weak version | Strong version |
|---|---|---|
| Background | "I am passionate about AI." | "Master's student in robotics; built a pick-and-place demo on a low-cost arm." |
| Past work | No links | 1–3 links to repositories, demos or videos, each with one line on what you built yourself |
| Goal for the event | "To learn and network." | "To train a navigation policy in simulation and test it on a quadruped." |
| Fit with tracks or theme | Not mentioned | Names the track you want and why your skills match it |
| Team contribution | "I can do anything." | "I handle perception and data; I am looking for someone strong in control." |
| Availability | Unclear | Confirms you can attend the full event |
| Completeness | Short or empty answers | Every question answered in plain, specific sentences |

The pattern is the same in every row: replace general statements with facts a reviewer can check in a few seconds.

## How do you show your skills if you have little experience?

Show small, finished projects instead of large, unfinished ones. A short repository with a clear README and a video of a robot or model doing one thing is stronger evidence than a long list of courses.

Ways to build evidence quickly:

- **Coursework with a demo.** Turn a course project into a public repository with a short video.
- **A weekend project.** Train a small model, run a simulation or control a low-cost robot, and write down what failed.
- **Open-source contributions.** Fix an issue or improve documentation in a robotics or ML project you use.
- **Research summaries.** If you are a researcher, link a paper or poster and explain in one sentence what you implemented.

At HackLab Robotics' Edition #1, attendees included master's students building their first real prototypes, so first prototypes are a normal starting point at a robotics hackathon. If you come from a field outside computer science, read [do you need to be a CS major to join](/articles/do-you-need-to-be-a-cs-major-to-join).

## How do you write the "what will you build" answer?

Write the "what will you build" answer as one concrete task, the tools you expect to use and the result you want to demo. Two or three sentences are enough.

A simple template:

1. **Task:** what the robot or system should do.
2. **Approach:** the method or tools you plan to use.
3. **Demo:** what judges will see working at the end.

Tie it to the event's tracks where possible. For HackLab Robotics' Edition #2 the tracks are AI Infrastructure, Industrial Robotics Simulation, Autonomous Navigation and Wildcard / Open Source; an answer that names one of them and fits your skills is easy to understand. For help choosing an idea, see [how do I choose what to build](/articles/how-do-i-choose-what-to-build).

## Should you apply alone or with a team?

Follow the event's rules on teams; if both are allowed, choose based on whether you already have teammates with complementary skills. Organisers set their own team rules, so read the event page before deciding.

If you apply alone, describe the role you want and the skills you are looking for in teammates. That makes it easier to place you. If you apply with others, make sure each person's application is complete on its own. For tips on forming a team at the event, see [how to find the right team](/articles/how-to-find-the-right-team).

## What are common mistakes in hackathon applications?

The most common mistakes are vague answers, missing links and late submissions. A checklist to run before you submit:

- Every question answered with a specific sentence, not a slogan.
- Links open, are public and point to the right project.
- Your role in each linked project is stated.
- Your goal matches the event's theme or tracks.
- Your availability for the full event is confirmed.
- Spelling of names, tools and your contact details is checked.
- Submitted before the deadline, ideally well before it.

If you are not selected, keep the application. Update it with what you build next and apply to the next event.

## What happens after you are accepted?

After acceptance, confirm your place quickly and start preparing. Selective events often have a waiting list, and an unconfirmed place may go to someone else.

Then set up your laptop, refresh the basics for your track and plan a small first goal for the robot or model. A hackathon project can also support a job search afterwards; see [will a hackathon help me get a job](/articles/will-a-hackathon-help-me-get-a-job). After HackLab Robotics' Edition #1, 5 participants were hired by sponsor companies.
