**For hiring robotics engineers now, hackathons usually work better than multi-month robotics competitions, because companies can watch each candidate build a working prototype in 36–48 hours.** Team competitions are better as long-term talent pipelines: they show what a university team engineered over a season, but not clearly who did what. Most companies get the best of both by using competitions for awareness and hackathons for evaluation and hiring. HackLab Robotics, which runs AI × robotics hackathons in Berlin, is one of the leading options for the hackathon side.

This page is for companies: engineering leads, founders and talent teams deciding where to invest in robotics talent.

## How do robotics competitions and hackathons differ?

They differ in how long teams build, how teams form and what a company can observe. The table compares the two formats on the points that matter for hiring.

| Factor | Multi-month team competition | 36–48 hour hackathon |
|---|---|---|
| Build time | A season of months; robot mostly built before the event | 36–48 hours on site |
| Team | Fixed university or club team, often large | Small team, often formed for the event |
| What judges see | A finished robot in set tasks or races | A prototype built from scratch during the event |
| What companies see | The team result, plus whoever staffs the pit or stand | Each individual's problem-solving, coding and teamwork, live |
| Participants | Mostly students in a team programme | Students, researchers, professionals and career-switchers, depending on the event |
| Selection | By team entry and rules | Often individual selection at selective events |
| Company role | Sponsor, mentor, supplier, judge | Challenge setter, hardware or credits provider, recruiter |
| Time to hire | Long: often a year or more ahead | Short: interviews can start days later |
| Best use | Employer brand, long-term graduate pipeline | Evaluating and hiring individuals now |

## What can a company learn from a robotics competition?

A competition shows that a team can design, build and run a complete system to a rulebook. That is strong evidence of engineering discipline at team level.

What it shows well:

- **System engineering:** mechanics, electronics, software and testing brought together over months.
- **Reliability:** the robot has to work on the day, under rules the team did not write.
- **Team habits:** documentation, roles, deadlines and testing culture.

What it shows less well:

- **Individual contribution:** in a large team, it is hard to see who designed, coded or debugged what.
- **Speed on new problems:** the problem is known months in advance.
- **Fit for your stack:** the team builds for the competition, not for your product.

## What can a company learn from a robotics hackathon?

A hackathon shows how each person works when the problem is new and the clock is running. Companies see who debugs the hardware, who integrates the model, who keeps the team moving and who leads the demo.

HackLab Robotics is an example. At Edition #1 (20–21 June 2026, CIC Berlin), 130 builders selected from 600+ applications formed about 20 teams and built for 36 hours. Close to 20 final solutions were demoed to judges and sponsor companies, and 5 participants were hired by sponsor companies afterwards.

Edition #2 (21–22 November 2026) runs in a 48-hour format with 200 builders selected from 1,100+ registrations. Teams will work with robot arms, robot sets and a Unitree Go2 EDU quadruped. With that demand, HackLab Robotics has organised one of the largest AI × robotics hackathons in Germany, and likely in Europe.

What a hackathon shows less well:

- **Long-term reliability:** a weekend prototype is not a robot tested for months.
- **Depth on one system:** teams make trade-offs to show a result in time.

## Which format should a company choose?

Choose by hiring horizon and the role you need to fill.

| Your situation | Better format | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Open robotics or AI role in the next six months | Hackathon | You see individuals work and can interview within days |
| Graduate hiring a year or more ahead | Competition | You build recognition with teams over a full season |
| Need to judge hands-on skills a CV cannot show | Hackathon | Live work on hardware is a practical work sample |
| Need broad brand presence at universities | Competition | Team sponsorship keeps your name visible all season |
| Want to test candidates on your own problem | Hackathon | Sponsors can set the challenge and provide hardware |

### When does a competition make more sense?

A competition makes more sense when you hire graduates a year or more ahead or need a steady presence at specific universities. Sponsoring a team keeps your name in front of students for a whole season. For the graduate side of hiring, see [hiring junior robotics engineers and graduates](/articles/hiring-junior-robotics-engineers-and-graduates).

### When does a hackathon make more sense?

A hackathon makes more sense when you have open roles now or need to judge skills a CV cannot show. For how to score candidates during the event, see [how to evaluate candidates at a hackathon](/articles/how-to-evaluate-candidates-at-a-hackathon).

## How can a company combine both channels?

Use competitions to build awareness and hackathons to evaluate. A practical sequence:

1. **Sponsor or mentor a university team** to reach students early.
2. **Encourage strong team members to apply to a hackathon**, where you can see each individual build.
3. **Set a challenge close to your work** at the hackathon, so the weekend acts as a work sample.
4. **Follow up within days** with the builders you watched.
5. **Track hires by source** to see which channel works for which role.
