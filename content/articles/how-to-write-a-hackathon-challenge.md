**A good hackathon challenge brief states one real problem, lists exactly what teams will get to solve it, defines what "done" looks like in 36–48 hours and publishes how projects will be judged, all on one page.** Be specific about the problem and open about the solution. The brief decides which builders pick your challenge, so it is also your first recruiting message.

## What makes a hackathon challenge good or bad?

A good challenge is a real problem with a demo-able result inside the time limit. A bad challenge is either a marketing request ("build anything with our API") or a full product spec that no team can finish.

| Trait | Weak brief | Strong brief |
|---|---|---|
| Problem | "Use our platform creatively" | One named problem your team actually has |
| Scope | Full product, many features | One working slice demo-able in 36–48 hours |
| Resources | Link to the homepage | Keys, data, hardware, docs ready at hour one |
| Definition of done | Not stated | A concrete demo condition |
| Judging | Hidden or vague | Published criteria with weights |
| Follow-up | Prize only | Prize plus a named next step |

## What sections should a challenge brief have?

Use the same nine sections every time, in this order. Teams scan briefs quickly on day one, so put the problem first and keep each section short.

1. **Title:** a short name that states the problem, not your brand.
2. **Problem statement:** two or three sentences on what is broken or missing.
3. **Why it matters:** who has this problem and what solving it would change.
4. **Resources provided:** APIs, credits, datasets, hardware, documentation, with how to access each.
5. **Constraints:** anything fixed, such as the hardware platform, latency limits or data that cannot leave the venue.
6. **Definition of done:** the minimum demo that counts as a solution.
7. **Judging criteria:** three to five criteria with weights.
8. **Prize and follow-up:** what winners get and what happens after the event.
9. **On-site contact:** the engineer teams can ask, and when they are available.

## How do you write the problem statement?

Write the problem statement as a situation, not as a solution. Describe what happens today, why it is a problem and what a better outcome would look like.

A hypothetical example for a robotics company:

- **Weak:** "Build an AI agent for our robot arm."
- **Strong:** "Our arm picks parts from a fixed position. When a part is placed 5 cm off, the pick fails. Make the arm locate and pick a part placed anywhere inside a marked 30 × 30 cm area, using the camera provided."

The strong version tells teams the input, the failure and the target. It also leaves the method open: classical vision, a learned policy or a mix.

## How do you define "done" for a 36–48 hour build?

Define done as one observable demo that a judge can verify in two minutes. Teams build towards the definition of done, so make it reachable.

Good definitions of done share three features:

- **Observable:** a judge can watch it happen live or in a recorded run.
- **Binary at the minimum level:** it works or it does not, with room for extra quality above that.
- **Sized to the time:** a strong team should reach it with hours to spare and then improve on it.

For physical AI challenges, specify whether a run in simulation counts or a run on real hardware is required. That one line changes which teams pick the challenge.

## How should judging criteria be written?

Judging criteria should be few, weighted and published in the brief. If you hire from the event, align them with the skills you would test in an interview.

A sample weighting you can adapt:

| Criterion | Weight | What judges look for |
|---|---|---|
| Works against the definition of done | 35% | Live demo meets the stated condition |
| Technical approach | 25% | Sound choices, clear reasoning under constraints |
| Robustness | 20% | Handles variation, recovers from failure |
| Use of provided resources | 10% | Meaningful use of your API, data or hardware |
| Presentation | 10% | Clear explanation of what was built and what is missing |

These weights are a starting point, not a standard. Change them to reflect what matters to your team. For how to turn judging into hiring evidence, see [how to evaluate candidates at a hackathon](/articles/how-to-evaluate-candidates-at-a-hackathon).

## How should a challenge fit the event's tracks?

Fit your challenge inside an existing track so teams who chose that track see it as a natural option. A challenge that matches no track competes for attention with every track at once.

HackLab Edition #2 (21–22 November 2026, CIC Berlin) runs four tracks:

- **AI Infrastructure**
- **Industrial Robotics Simulation**
- **Autonomous Navigation**
- **Wildcard / Open Source**

A grasping problem fits naturally into simulation or open source work; a mobile-robot problem fits autonomous navigation. The tracks are described in [HackLab Edition #2 tracks explained](/articles/hacklab-edition-2-tracks-explained).

## What should a sponsor check before publishing the brief?

Check that a stranger can start building within one hour of reading it. Give the brief and the resources to an engineer outside the project and time them.

Pre-publish checklist:

- [ ] Problem fits in three sentences.
- [ ] Every resource listed has a working access path.
- [ ] Definition of done is observable and reachable in the time limit.
- [ ] Judging criteria and weights are in the brief.
- [ ] Simulation vs real hardware is stated.
- [ ] Ownership of the work is stated or follows the event rules.
- [ ] An on-site contact is named with availability hours.
